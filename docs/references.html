Modal Synthesis and Structural Listening

Ayers, Lydia, and Andrew Horner. 2005. “Synthesizing a Javanese Gong Ageng.” In Proceedings of the International Computer Music Conference 2005, 644–647. Its reduced additive model demonstrates how selected harmonic and inharmonic landmarks, differentiated amplitude envelopes, and synchronized slow/fast AM and FM can keep a small sustained modal set perceptually active. Ambi Encoder Modal generalizes those techniques across fictional body families; it does not claim to model the analyzed instrument. ICMC proceedings paper.

Bilbao, Stefan. 2005. “Sound Synthesis for Nonlinear Plates.” In Proceedings of the 8th International Conference on Digital Audio Effects, 243–248. Its finite-difference plate model demonstrates nonlinear modal coupling, frequency-dependent damping, and size-dependent transfer of energy toward higher frequencies. Those relationships inform Ambi Encoder Modal’s bounded sustained evolution rather than a literal real-time plate solver. DAFx paper.

Canfield-Dafilou, Elliot Kermit, and Kurt James Werner. 2017. “Modal Audio Effects: A Carillon Case Study.” In Proceedings of the 20th International Conference on Digital Audio Effects, 247–254. The decaying-sinusoid analysis and parametric resynthesis framework supports the instrument's general modal architecture rather than a carillon emulation. Publication record and paper.

Carvalho, Miguel, Vincent Debut, and Jose Antunes. 2021. “Physical Modelling Techniques for the Dynamical Characterization and Sound Synthesis of Historical Bells.” Heritage Science 9: 157. Its paired modes, differentiated damping, asymmetric observation, and time-domain synthesis provide general strategies for sustaining and reading modal structures. The resulting fictional body families are not historical-bell models. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40494-021-00620-2.

Cho, Sangjin. 2015. “Acoustic Analysis of a Jing Based on Drive Point and Blow Strength.” Journal of the Acoustical Society of Korea 34 (4): 328–334. The reported modal spacing and late beating pairs offer general evidence for slowly evolving resonant spectra. Ambi Encoder Modal abstracts those relationships without identifying a body as the measured instrument. Journal article and full text.

Christian, Richard S., Robert E. Davis, Arnold Tubis, Craig A. Anderson, Ronald I. Mills, and Thomas D. Rossing. 1984. “Effects of Air Loading on Timpani Membrane Vibrations.” Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 76 (5): 1336–1345. Its measured and calculated modal frequencies and decay times demonstrate that an enclosure loads different membrane modes differently; this informs Cavity Membrane’s abstract air-loaded branches rather than a timpani model. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.391449.

Ducceschi, Michele, Cyril Touzé, and Stefan Bilbao. 2013. “Sound Synthesis of Gongs Obtained from Nonlinear Thin Plates Vibrations: Comparison Between a Modal Approach and a Finite Difference Scheme.” In SMAC Stockholm Music Acoustics Conference 2013. Its independently tunable modal damping, modal-truncation analysis, nonlinear high-frequency energy cascade, and sparse symmetry rules motivate the instrument’s bounded sustained coupling and shimmer, not an exact physical reconstruction. HAL paper and PDF.

Friends of Notre-Dame de Paris. 2022. “The Silent Sounds of Notre-Dame’s Bells.” Documents Bill Fontana’s accelerometer installation and its use of environmental energy to disclose the bells’ otherwise silent harmonic activity. Project account.

Han, Je-Seok. 2013. “A Study on Close Microphone Techniques for Korean Traditional Musical Instruments (Gayageum, Haegeum, Daegeum, and Kkwaenggwari).” Journal of Korea Academia-Industrial Cooperation Society 14 (10): 4753–4761. Its measurements illustrate that contact and radiation perspectives can emphasize very different frequency regions. That distinction informs the generic Contact–Radiation control, not a calibrated model of any named instrument. https://doi.org/10.5762/KAIS.2013.14.10.4753.

Kim, Youngmin, and Jun Kim. 2010. “A Study on the Harmonic Structure and Timbral Change by Playing Style of the Kkwaenggwari.” Journal of the Korean Society of Safety, Health and Arts 2 (1): 1–8. Its analysis supports the broader design principle that related resonators can retain distinct modal balances and timbral evolution. Ambi Encoder Modal applies that principle to fictional bright-body profiles and sustained presets. Article record.

Mutable Instruments. 2014. Elements. Open-source modal synthesis instrument by Émilie Gillet. Its 64-mode resonator, geometry-dependent partial stretching, frequency-dependent Q, bowed low-mode feedback, interpolated pickup position, and separation of actuation from resonator state inform Ambi Encoder Modal’s sustained real-time architecture. Elements source repository.

Ramakrishna, B. S., and Man Mohan Sondhi. 1954. “Vibrations of Indian Musical Drums Regarded as Composite Membranes.” Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 26 (4): 523–529. Its theory and measurements show how a radial density discontinuity changes a circular membrane’s eigenvalues and approximate degeneracies; Loaded Membrane uses that general added-mass principle without reproducing the measured drums. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1907369.

Suzuki, Hideo, and Yun-Fan Hwang. 2008. “Coupling between Two Membranes of a Japanese Drum.” Acoustical Science and Technology 29 (3): 215–220. Its analysis of two slightly mistuned circular membranes supports Coupled Membrane’s paired lattices and slow energy exchange, not an emulation of the studied instrument. https://doi.org/10.1250/ast.29.215.

Worland, Randy. 2010. “Normal Modes of a Musical Drumhead under Non-Uniform Tension.” Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 127 (1): 525–533. Its measurements and finite-element analysis show how nonuniform tension alters ideal circular-membrane modes and splits nominally degenerate pairs; this informs the Tensioned Skin and Loose Membrane abstractions. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3268605.
